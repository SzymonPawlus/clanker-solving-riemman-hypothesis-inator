# 2026-08-29 — inscribed equilateral triangles, the convex case (issue #132)

Lane: `problems/inscribed-equilateral-triangle/attacks/convex-vertex-criterion/`. Files owned:
that directory's `README.md` and `KILL-CRITERION.md`, and this entry. Ran no git commands.

## What I was given

Four claims (C1)–(C4) about which points `O` of a convex curve `J = ∂K` are vertices of an
inscribed equilateral triangle, keyed on the interior angle `α(O)`, plus a sketch of each and an
instruction to break them first.

## Order of events

**1. Fixed the definitions before touching the claims.** Defined `α(O)` as the opening of
`T(O) = closure of the cone generated by K − O`. With that definition `K ⊆ O + T(O)` is one line,
which is worth noticing: the brief warned that this containment "is the whole argument" and that
I should check it carefully. It is trivially true — *but only for that definition*. If you define
the tangent cone by secant limits or by tangent lines you owe a proof. So the real risk is a
definition mismatch between the statement and the proof, not the containment. I flagged that in
K3 of the kill-criterion rather than in the proof, because that is where a reviewer will look.

**2. (C1) fell out immediately and is robust.** The strong form is `P,Q ∈ K\{O} ⟹ ∠POQ ≤ α(O)`,
from the containment plus "two vectors in a sector of opening `≤ π` subtend at most that
opening". Needs `α ≤ π`, which needs the supporting line, which needs `O ∈ ∂K`. Fine.

**3. (C3) — I threw away the briefed route.** The brief wanted total turning `= 2π` and exterior
angles. Making that precise for a general convex curve costs either a restriction to polygons or
a turning measure. Instead: three points with `α < π/3` are non-collinear (a middle point would
see the other two at angle `π`), so they form a triangle whose angles are each `< π/3`, summing
to `< π`. Contradiction. Two lines, no rectifiability, no polygon restriction, and it strengthens
to `α ≤ π/3` with at most three such points which then force `K` to be an equilateral triangle.
This is the cleanest thing in the write-up and I am fairly confident in it.

**4. (C2) is where the day actually went.** Three distinct things happened, in this order.

*(a) I produced a fluent counterexample to (C2) that was wrong.* I set up the reduction "`O` good
iff `∃θ` with `r(θ) = r(θ − π/3)`" using the radial function `r` from `O`, then took the thin
triangle `O, (δ,0), L·e^{i(π/3+ε)}` with `δ ≪ L` and observed that `F(θ) = r(θ) − r(θ−π/3)` is
strictly positive on the whole range, so no solution — apparently killing (C2) for `α` strictly
above `π/3`. It read perfectly. It is wrong: on an **extreme ray** of the tangent cone the entire
radial segment `(0, r(θ)]` lies on `∂K`, not just its far endpoint, so the condition is
`Σ(θ) ∩ Σ(θ−π/3) ≠ ∅`, not `r(θ) = r(θ−π/3)`. The thin triangle is good, with `P` partway up the
long edge. What caught it was trying to *build* the witness triangle explicitly instead of
trusting the sign of `F`. That is the §0 failure mode arriving exactly on schedule, and I want to
record that the thing that saved me was constructing an object, not re-reading an argument.

*(b) (C2) then turned out to be genuinely false, but at a different place.* `α = π/3` exactly.
`K* = {0 ≤ x ≤ 1, x² ≤ y ≤ √3 x}` at the origin: the tangent cone is the closed sector `[0, π/3]`
because it is a *closure*, but the achieved-direction set is `A = (0, π/3]` — the parabola never
actually points along the `x`-axis. Two directions in `A` can never differ by exactly `π/3`, so
no inscribed equilateral triangle has a vertex at the origin. The mechanism is that
`α` is a property of the closure and the conclusion is a property of `K`. I like this
counterexample: it is exact, it is one line to check, and it explains itself.

*(c) The repair is an iff, not a retreat.* `α > π/3` ⟹ good, always; `α = π/3` ⟹ good iff `A` is
closed (both extreme rays meet `K` in a real segment); `α < π/3` ⟹ never. For polygons `A` is
always closed, so (C2) is true verbatim for convex polytopes — the brief's statement is a correct
theorem about polytopes with a hypothesis too weak for general convex bodies.

**5. The existence proof.** Three cases on whether `r(0) ≥ r(π/3)`, whether
`r(α) ≥ r(α−π/3)`, and otherwise an IVT on `F(θ) = r(θ) − r(θ−π/3)` over `(π/3, α)`, using that
`r` is continuous on the open interval `(0,α)` and upper semicontinuous at the ends. The two
extreme-ray cases are exactly the ones I would have missed if not for step 4(a); the equilateral
triangle itself sits in Case A/B and is invisible to the IVT.

**6. I dropped the brief's IVT and it cost nothing.** The brief wanted the IVT along `J \ {O}` on
`P ↦ gauge(ρ(P)) − 1`, which needs `J \ {O}` connected, i.e. `∂K ≅ S¹`. Running the IVT on the
angle instead needs only the IVT on a real interval. So the whole file can honestly say: no
Jordan curve theorem, no degree argument, no rectifiability, no smoothness. Two corrections to
the brief fell out of comparing them: the "outside" end of the brief's argument is free and needs
no hypothesis whatsoever (it holds for every `α ∈ (0,π]`), and the "inside" end is not a matter
of strictness — for the equilateral triangle with `O` a vertex, `ρ(P) ∈ int K` holds for **no**
`P` at all, so at `α = π/3` the IVT has nothing to work with no matter how it is set up.

**7. (C4).** Exact in sympy: the `30–30–120` triangle `(0,0),(1,0),(−1/2,√3/2)` has the inscribed
equilateral triangle `(0,0), (0, 1/√3), (1/2, 1/(2√3))` of side `√3/3`, with the two new vertices
at parameters `1/3` and `2/3` along the long edge. Both `30°` corners fail by (C1). Exactly two
non-good points, bound attained. `K*` from step 4(b) is a second, structurally different witness
for the bound (one failure by `α < π/3`, one by the new `α = π/3` mechanism).

**8. Non-transfer to squares.** The right statement is not about `60°` being special, it is
about counting: an equilateral triangle with a vertex at `O` is fixed by one more point and has
one remaining vertex — one equation, one unknown, IVT. A square with a corner at `O` is fixed by
one more point and has *two* remaining corners — two equations, one unknown, overdetermined. The
method transcribed to `π/2` proves "every convex curve carries a right isoceles triangle with its
apex anywhere the angle exceeds `π/2`", which is true and is not the square peg problem.
Numerically: for the equilateral triangle, `0` of `399` sampled points on a side are corners of
an inscribed square, and the classical square is recovered exactly at `x = (2−√3)/2`. A triangle
has exactly three inscribed squares (pigeonhole: four corners on three sides), so at most twelve
of uncountably many boundary points qualify — the "all but two" statement is flatly false for
squares. I put this in the kill-criterion as a trap (K5) rather than only as prose, because it is
the check I would most want a future me to actually run.

## Numerics, and a second wrong thing

400 seeded random convex polygons, ~1800 vertices: criterion agreed with a bisection search
everywhere, and no polygon had more than two vertices with `α < π/3`.

Then an independent boundary-walk checker disagreed on ~200 vertices. My first instinct was that
Theorem B was wrong. It was a Python closure bug: `s` was bound to `sin(−60°)` and then
reassigned by an arclength loop variable, so the rotation was being applied with a garbage sine.
After fixing it, two residual disagreements remained, both because I had excluded a 2%-of-
perimeter window around `O` to suppress the degenerate root and the genuine inscribed triangles
there had side `0.9%` and `1.6%` of the perimeter. Both were confirmed by hand as real. So: two
separate occasions today where a *checker* produced an apparent refutation of a true statement.
That is the same lesson as step 4(a) from the other direction — a disagreement between a claim
and a check is at least as likely to be a bug in the check.

Numerics were run in scratch and **not committed**: `experiments/` belongs to another worker on
#132 and `RULES.md` §2 is structural. Noted in the README so nobody thinks they are citable.

## Weakest step

**The endpoint estimates in Case C of Theorem B(i)** — specifically the reliance on `r` being
*only* upper semicontinuous at `θ = 0` and `θ = α`, with the inequalities
`limsup_{ψ→0⁺} r(ψ) ≤ r(0)` and `limsup_{θ→α⁻} r(θ) ≤ r(α)` pointing in the direction that makes
`F > 0` near `π/3⁺` and `F < 0` near `α⁻`. If either semicontinuity is the wrong way round, or if
I have mixed up which end needs which, Case C collapses and with it the whole existence half for
general convex bodies (the polygon case would survive, since there `r` is continuous on the
closed interval). That is the step I would formalise first and the one I would hand a
cross-examiner first.
